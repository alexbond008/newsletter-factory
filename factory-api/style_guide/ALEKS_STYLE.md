# Aleks Gornik — Newsletter Style Guide

## Who You Are Writing For
Young engineers aged 18–28. Primarily students studying electrical engineers, but also mechanical, civil, computer engineering
They are not failing — they are vaguely stuck. Ambitious but slightly anxious about whether they made
the right career choices. Curious, self-aware, want to level up but aren't sure how.

## Voice and Tone
- Second-person throughout: "you", "your". Never "readers", "engineers", or "people".
- Conversational and peer-to-peer. Aleks is slightly ahead of the reader, not a distant guru.
- Self-effacing and honest. Aleks admits his own confusion, wrong turns, and moments of doubt.
- No hype. No corporate jargon. No coaching-speak ("unlock your potential", "show up as your best self").
- No LinkedIn tone. If it could be posted by a stranger on LinkedIn, rewrite it.
- Dry humour is welcome. Performative enthusiasm is not.
- Be direct. "Most engineers are doing this wrong." is better than "Many engineers may find it helpful to consider..."
- Short sentences. Vary paragraph length. Never more than 4 sentences in a paragraph.

## Mandatory Post Structure (follow this exactly)

### 1. HOOK (2–4 sentences)
Opens with ONE of:
- A personal confession or failure ("I spent 18 months thinking X was the answer. I was wrong.")
- A provocative claim that creates identity anxiety ("You picked your degree for all the wrong reasons.")
- An external reference that raises stakes (a podcast, a news event, something that happened at work)

The hook must create immediate resonance. The reader should think "that's me" or "I need to know more" within the first two sentences.

### 2. CORE FRAMEWORK (3–5 sections)
The actual insight. Use a numbered list, named framework, or clear categories when the content supports it.
Each item: 1–2 sentences of explanation + optional brief story or example.
Concrete specifics beat abstract principles. "I dropped game dev after reading about 80-hour crunch weeks" beats "research the day-to-day reality".

### 3. CLOSE (1–2 paragraphs)
A moment of reassurance or personal reflection. What this meant for Aleks. How it connects back to the reader's life.
NO hard sell. Never "sign up now", "don't miss out", "act today".
End on something the reader can sit with.

### 4. CTA STACK (always last, always in this exact order — no exceptions)
- Book a 1:1 with Aleks: https://calendly.com/aleksgornik/30min
- Subscribe to YouTube: @aleksgornik
- "Hit reply — I read every email."

## Subject Line Formula
Generate exactly 3 subject line options. Every subject line must be a question.

Two proven formats:
- Identity question: "Did You Pick the Wrong Engineering Degree?"
- Self-classification question: "The 4 types of engineers (Which one are you?)"

Rules:
- Always second-person ("you", "your") or first-person confession ("I'm Not the Engineer I Thought I Was")
- Use emotionally honest words: "terrifying", "wrong", "not", "even"
- Promise exactly what the post delivers — no clickbait
- Never a declarative statement as a subject line

## Preview Text
One sentence. Shown in the email inbox preview. Should tease the core framework or personal confession without giving it away.

## Length and Format
- Body: 600–900 words
- Paragraphs: 2–4 sentences max
- No section headers inside the body — structure is implicit, not labeled
- Output format: HTML with <p> tags wrapping each paragraph only
- No <h1>, <h2>, <strong>, <em>, or other formatting tags in the body
- The CTA stack uses <p> tags like everything else

## What the Data Shows Works
- Posts about identity and self-classification outperform information posts (44% vs 27% open rate)
- Personal confession in the first sentence outperforms leading with a general claim
- Specific numbers and named categories outperform vague advice
- Questions in subject lines consistently outperform statements
- Keeping unsub rate under 0.5% requires the content to feel like it was written for this specific reader

## What to Avoid
- "However", "It's important to note", "It's worth mentioning" — hedge phrases Aleks never uses
- Passive voice
- Lists of bullet points in the body (use narrative instead)
- Ending on the CTA without a proper close paragraph first
- Repeating the subject line verbatim in the opening sentence
