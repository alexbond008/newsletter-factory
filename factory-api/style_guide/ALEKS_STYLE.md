# Aleks Gornik — Newsletter Style Guide

## Who You Are Writing For
Engineering students and recent graduates aged 18–25. Ambitious but overwhelmed. They are not failing — they're stuck between where they are and where they want to be. They battle imposter syndrome, confusion about career paths, and the gap between what university teaches and what the real world demands. They want an older brother who's been through it and will tell them the truth, not a coach selling them a course.

Core pain points: failing difficult modules, confusion about coding languages, lost in job hunting, imposter syndrome, burnout.

## Voice and Tone
- Second-person throughout: "you", "your". Never "readers", "engineers", or "people".
- Brutally honest, no-BS, energetic, deeply relatable. Older brother/mentor energy — not guru energy.
- Encouraging but never sugarcoating. Confident, direct, human.
- Self-effacing: Aleks admits his own confusion, wrong turns, and moments of panic.
- No hype. No corporate jargon. No coaching-speak ("unlock your potential", "show up as your best self").
- No LinkedIn tone. If a stranger could post it without changing a word, rewrite it.
- Dry humour welcome. Performative enthusiasm is not.
- Always use contractions: "you're", "it's", "don't", "wasn't". Never formal alternatives.

## Craft Principles (these determine quality)

### Opening sentences — the most important part
The first sentence must create immediate friction: a counterintuitive claim, a vivid embarrassing scene, or a blunt confession that makes the reader feel slightly implicated. Use one of:

**Counterintuitive reversal (Manson-style):** State a widely held belief, then contradict it immediately.
> "Everyone says internships are essential. I applied to mine because I wanted better grades."

**Vivid specific scene (Ferriss-style):** Drop into a real moment with concrete details — names, places, numbers.
> "I was staring at a C problem on screen, a Belarusian engineer from Intel watching me freeze, and I had absolutely no idea what I was doing."

**Blunt confession:** Lead with a failure or embarrassing truth without softening it.
> "My CV was genuinely bad. I still got the interviews."

Never open with a general statement about engineering, career advice, or the topic of the post. Jump straight into the moment or the reversal.

### Sentence rhythm
Short sentences are truth drops. Long sentences carry evidence and explanation.
- Short (4–8 words): make a definitive claim — "Action beats strategy every time."
- Long (20+ words): immediately follow with the context that earns it.
- Never write three long sentences in a row. Break with something short and punchy.
- If a sentence can be cut in half without losing meaning, cut it.

### Transitions
Forbidden: "however", "furthermore", "in conclusion", "it's worth noting", "it's important to mention", "that being said".
Use instead: "Here's the thing...", "But here's what nobody tells you...", "And that's the point.", "The truth is...", "Which is exactly why...", "What that taught me was..."

Transitions should read like the next thought in a spoken conversation, not a paragraph in a report.

### Concreteness — the most common failure mode
Every abstract principle needs one specific anchor from the transcript. Not "I failed some interviews" but "I froze on a C problem at Intel and told the interviewer I couldn't do it." The specific detail is what makes the lesson land and feel real.

Pattern: specific story → name the principle it illustrates → apply it to the reader's situation.

### Cleaning transcript language
The transcript is raw speech. It must be rewritten, not cleaned up. Common patterns to fix:
- "I was probably in around 15 recruitment cycles" → "I went through 15 recruitment processes"
- "I think that was a good thing" → cut the hedge, state it directly
- "you know what I mean" → delete
- Filler words to remove: "basically", "sort of", "kind of", "honestly" (unless clearly rhetorical), "I think", "you know"
- Run-on transcript sentences must be broken into 2–3 short punchy ones

## Mandatory Post Structure

### 1. HOOK (2–4 sentences)
One of: counterintuitive reversal, vivid scene with specific detail, blunt confession.
Reader must think "that's me" or "I need to know why" within the first two sentences.
Do NOT start with a generic statement about engineering or career advice.

### 2. CORE (3–5 paragraphs)
The actual insight, grounded entirely in specific stories and details from the transcript.
Use a named framework or numbered categories when the content supports it — they are skimmable and memorable.
Each section: specific story/example from transcript → named principle → what it means for the reader.
Challenge the reader directly: "Ask yourself...", "You've probably done this.", "What's your version of this?"

### 3. CLOSE (1–2 paragraphs)
Personal reflection on what this meant for Aleks. How it changed the way he operates.
Connect back to the reader — make them feel understood, not lectured.
End with a challenge or a question they can sit with. No hard sell. No "don't miss out."

### 4. CTA STACK (always last, always in this exact order — no exceptions)
- Book a 1:1 with Aleks: https://calendly.com/aleksgornik/30min
- Subscribe to YouTube: @aleksgornik
- "Hit reply — I read every email."

## Subject Line Formula
Generate exactly 3 subject line options.

Proven formats:
- **Counterintuitive paradox**: "The Worst Reason to Apply for an Internship (I Did It Anyway)"
- **Identity question**: "Did You Pick the Wrong Engineering Degree?"
- **Blunt confession as question**: "Are You Applying for Internships for the Wrong Reasons?"
- **Self-classification**: "The 4 Types of Engineers (Which One Are You?)"

Rules:
- Second-person ("you", "your") or first-person confession ("I'm Not the Engineer I Thought I Was")
- Emotionally honest words: "terrifying", "wrong", "not", "even", "worst", "real", "actually"
- Promise exactly what the post delivers — no clickbait
- Questions consistently outperform statements

## Preview Text
One sentence. Tease the central paradox or confession without giving away the resolution. Make the reader feel like they might be making a mistake they don't yet know about.

## Length and Format
- Body: 600–900 words
- Paragraphs: 2–4 sentences max. Never more.
- No section headers inside the body — structure is implicit
- Output format: HTML with `<p>` tags wrapping each paragraph only
- No `<h1>`, `<h2>`, `<strong>`, `<em>`, or other tags in the body
- CTA stack uses `<p>` tags like everything else

## What the Data Shows Works
- Identity/self-classification posts: 40–44% open rate vs ~27% average
- Personal confession in sentence one beats any general opening
- Specific numbers, names, and categories beat vague advice every time
- Questions in subject lines beat statements consistently
- "All you need is one offer" style reassurance drives replies more than tactical tips

## What to Avoid
- Transcript speech patterns left uncleaned ("I was probably in around...")
- Hedging: "I think", "sort of", "kind of", "maybe", "perhaps"
- Passive voice
- Bullet point lists inside the body paragraphs — use narrative
- Ending on the CTA without a proper close paragraph first
- Generic advice that could appear in any career blog
- Sentences that trail off without landing on a clear point
- Any phrase that sounds like it came from a life coach or a LinkedIn post
